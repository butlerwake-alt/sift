How it works

Backtest vs forward test

A backtest replays a strategy's rules on past prices. It shows how the rules would have done, but the past was already known when the rules were chosen. A forward test runs the same rules from a start date onward, one new close at a time, so nobody can see the result in advance. Each card's big number and chart are the forward test. Backtest figures appear only on the strategy page, under their own heading.

$10,000 paper per strategy

Every strategy has its own simulated $10,000 account. Accounts never share money, use no leverage and place no real orders. There are no exchange keys. The dashed line on each chart is what simply buying and holding the benchmark would have done over the same dates.

Fills and costs

Daily strategies decide on one closing price and trade at the next close, so a signal is never filled at a price that was already known. The intraday test uses 5-minute bars and is flat by the close. Every trade pays a cost: the older tests use about 0.10% per side, and strategies 11–26 use 0.05% for stocks and 0.25% for crypto. Each strategy page lists its exact rules and costs.

How results update

Each paper engine records a mark after the market closes. Stocks close at 4 PM ET; crypto uses the daily candle that closes at 7 PM ET (midnight UTC). The board is rebuilt from those records, and existing marks are never rewritten. If a strategy's records stop updating, its card is marked stale.

What “proven in backtest” means

A strategy earns this label only if it passed five checks set before the test: profitable in both halves of the history, up in at least 60% of years, risk-adjusted results at least close to buy-and-hold, still profitable when its settings are nudged, and at least 15 trades. It says nothing about the future. “New forward-test hybrid” marks strategies that combine ideas and are being tried forward for the first time.

Known limits

Stock and coin lists are today's large names, so the backtests carry survivorship bias: companies that shrank or failed are missing. Short forward windows prove little; a few weeks of marks can easily be luck. Free public price data can have gaps, and filled gaps are logged.

Glossary

Paper trading
Simulated trading with pretend money, marked at real prices.
Forward test
Running fixed rules from a start date onward on new data only.
Backtest
Replaying rules on past prices to see how they would have done.
Buy-and-hold
Buying the benchmark once at the start and doing nothing else. The yardstick on every chart.
Drawdown
The drop from an account's highest value to a later low, in percent. “Max drawdown” is the worst one.
CAGR
Compound annual growth rate: the steady yearly return that would turn the start value into the end value.
Sharpe ratio
Return divided by how bumpy the ride was (volatility), per year. Higher is smoother; about 1 is good for a simple strategy.
Win rate
The share of closed trades that made money. A low win rate can still be profitable if the winners are big.
Profit factor
Total gains on winning trades divided by total losses on losing trades. Above 1 means it made more than it lost.
Market regime
A daily label for the market's state (trend up, range, risk-off, trend down, shock) from closing prices only.
Moving average
The average closing price over the last N days, such as the 50-day or 200-day average.
RSI
Relative Strength Index. A 2-day RSI under 10 means the last two days were sharply down.
Breakout
A close above the highest close of a past window, such as a 55-day high.
Momentum
The tendency of recent winners to keep winning for a while. Measured here as past return over a set window.
Out-of-sample
Data the rules were not built on. The second half of each backtest is reported separately for this reason.
Survivorship bias
Testing only on names that are large today, which leaves out the ones that failed and flatters results.
Slippage and fees
The cost of trading: the gap between the quoted and filled price plus commissions. Charged on every simulated trade.
Percentage points
The plain difference between two percentages: +3% versus +1% is 2 points ahead.
